# The geometry in the grove {#sec:geometry}

Everything in this section is drawn from the code both games run and is checked
by the repository's tests ([@sec:verification]). Volumes are in tetravolumes:
the regular tetrahedron whose edge is the unit of length holds one.

## Quadray coordinates {#sec:quadrays}

Four unit rays run from the centre of a regular tetrahedron to its corners.
They sum to zero, and every pair meets at the tetrahedral angle, whose cosine
is $-\tfrac{1}{3}$. Any point is a non-negative mix of the four rays, written
with at least one of the four values at zero. With the rays $q_i$ as unit
vectors, a point $P$ has the quadray

$$
\alpha_i = \tfrac{3}{4}\,(P \cdot q_i) - \min_j \tfrac{3}{4}\,(P \cdot q_j),
\qquad P = \sum_i \alpha_i\, q_i ,
$$ {#eq:quadray}

and subtracting the same amount from all four changes nothing, because the rays
sum to zero. The field station's compass shows the player's position relative
to the Ring this way. The lattice uses QuadCraft's convention instead, whose
rays point at alternate corners of a cube and are $\sqrt{3/2}$ long
[@quadcraft]; [@sec:conventions] relates the conventions.

## The isotropic vector matrix {#sec:ivm}

The isotropic vector matrix is the face-centred cubic arrangement of points,
each joined to its {{IVM_MOVES}} nearest neighbours by equal struts. It fills
space with tetrahedra and octahedra together, the octet truss [@fuller1975].
The field station's stillness lattice is a ball of it {{LATTICE_RADIUS}} m in
radius with struts {{LATTICE_EDGE}} m long: {{LATTICE_NODES}} nodes and
{{LATTICE_STRUTS}} struts. It snaps to its own period, so it stays fixed in
the world while the player turns.

Every node has an address: its quadray in QuadCraft's convention, with one
strut as two units and the foot of the Ring as $(0,0,0,0)$. Built axis-aligned,
the lattice sits in QuadCraft's own frame, so every address is whole.
Neighbouring addresses differ by one of QuadMath's {{IVM_MOVES}} neighbour
moves, the distinct permutations of $(2,1,1,0)$ [@quadmath].

## Tetravolumes, counted exactly {#sec:tetravolume}

The tally counts a tetrahedron with Tom Ace's determinant on the quadrays
$(a_k, b_k, c_k, d_k)$ of its four corners, as QuadMath implements it
[@quadmath]:

$$
V = \frac{1}{4}\left|\det\begin{pmatrix}
a_0 & b_0 & c_0 & d_0 & 1\\
a_1 & b_1 & c_1 & d_1 & 1\\
a_2 & b_2 & c_2 & d_2 & 1\\
a_3 & b_3 & c_3 & d_3 & 1\\
1 & 1 & 1 & 1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}\right| .
$$ {#eq:ace}

For integer quadrays the determinant is computed exactly, by Bareiss's
fraction-free elimination [@bareiss1968] in arbitrary-precision integers, and
$V$ is returned as a reduced fraction. The unit tetrahedron of the lattice, the
origin and three neighbour moves, holds {{UNIT_IVM_VOLUME}}.

## Why every count is whole {#sec:whole}

Mark any four nodes and the counter clicks out a whole number, or nothing when
the four lie in one plane. Integer coordinates alone would not ensure this.
QuadMath's primitive tetrahedron, the origin and three of the basis tips, holds
${{PRIMITIVE_TEX}}$; and among {{FIXTURE_INTEGER}} tetrahedra with arbitrary
integer quadrays, {{INT_FRACTIONAL}} come out fractional, while none of
{{FIXTURE_IVM}} tetrahedra built from lattice moves does ([@fig:counts]).

The reason is the lattice. The ${{ACE_ORDER}}\times{{ACE_ORDER}}$ determinant
in [@eq:ace] equals, up to sign, the $3\times 3$ determinant of the
tetrahedron's edge vectors written as $(a-d,\ b-d,\ c-d)$ [@quadmath]. Every
step between nodes is a sum of neighbour moves, and in that projection the
moves span the lattice generated by $(1,-1,0)$, $(1,0,-1)$ and $(2,1,1)$,
whose determinant is {{LATTICE_DET}}. Any three edge vectors between nodes
therefore have a determinant that is a multiple of {{LATTICE_DET}}, which is
exactly the divisor in [@eq:ace], and the tetravolume is whole. The tests mark
{{TALLY_SAMPLES}} random sets of four nodes on the stillness lattice and confirm
that each count is whole and equals the tetrahedron's ordinary volume divided
by that of a regular tetrahedron with the strut as its edge.

## The cube {#sec:cube}

Take the tetrahedron whose edges are the face diagonals of a cube. The cube
holds {{CUBE_VOLUME}} of it: the tetrahedron itself, and {{CUBE_CORNERS}}
trirectangular corner pieces of ${{CUBE_CORNER_TEX}}$ each,

$$
{{CUBE_VOLUME}} = {{TET_VOLUME}} + {{CUBE_CORNERS}} \times {{CUBE_CORNER_TEX}} .
$$

The tests measure this twice, once from ordinary volumes and once with
[@eq:ace] on QuadCraft quadrays, whose basis tips are the game's tetrahedron.
When an incursion resolves, the cube's edges fade, the inscribed tetrahedron
stays, and the corner pieces fly off. Their edges meet at right angles, so the
right angles leave the grove with them.

## The jitterbug and the Ring's gauge {#sec:jitterbug}

Fuller's jitterbug is the vector equilibrium's eight triangles, joined at their
corners, turning and closing through the icosahedron to the octahedron
[@fuller1975]. The games build it from the cyclic permutations of
$(0, \pm a, \pm b)$, scaled so that every triangle keeps the same edge. At
$a/b = 1$ the shape is the vector equilibrium; at
$a/b = 1/\varphi \approx {{INV_PHI}}$ it is the icosahedron, where each square
gap has closed into a pair of triangles; and at $a = 0$ it is the octahedron,
where the vertices meet in pairs.

Inside the Ring the compass rim becomes a dial that reads the jitterbug's
volume as it breathes. The gauge sums cones from the centre to the eight
triangles and the six squares, each square folded on its shorter diagonal, and
counts each cone with [@eq:ace] on QuadCraft quadrays. At the named stations it
reads {{VE_VOLUME}}, $5\sqrt{2}\,\varphi^{2} \approx {{ICOSA3}}$ and
{{OCTA_VOLUME}}. Between them the folded squares make the gauge read the
volume of the convex hull of its vertices, which the tests confirm
against an independent hull computation.

That reading is not monotone ([@fig:jitterbug]). Leaving the vector
equilibrium, the hull first swells, to {{HULL_PEAK}} near
$a/b = {{HULL_PEAK_AT}}$, and only then falls through the icosahedron to the
octahedron. The named stations step down in volume; the path between them,
measured this way, does not. The dial's full scale is taken from the same
sweep.

![The Ring's gauge across the jitterbug, from the octahedron to the vector equilibrium. Dots mark the named stations; the diamond marks the largest convex hull. Measured by the games' own code at {{SWEEP_POINTS}} evenly spaced values of the ratio.](../../output/figures/fig_jitterbug.png){#fig:jitterbug width=96%}

The ash that drifts over the field station falls in tiny vector equilibria
built from the jitterbug at its widest: eight triangles and six squares
closing {{VE_VOLUME}} tetravolumes, which the tests rebuild and measure.

## A modules {#sec:amodules}

Fuller cut the regular tetrahedron into identical slivers he called A quanta
modules [@fuller1975]. Each joins the tetrahedron's centre, one corner, the
midpoint of an edge at that corner, and the centre of a face holding both. There
is one for every such choice of face, corner and edge, {{A_MODULE_COUNT}} in
all. The tests place the tetrahedron's corners on quadrays scaled so that every
centre and midpoint is a whole quadray, and confirm with [@eq:ace] that each
module holds exactly ${{A_MODULE_SHARE_TEX}}$ of the tetrahedron. Half turn one
way and half the other, {{A_MODULE_HAND}} of each, so the modules come in
mirror pairs ([@fig:amodules]). At Public Works in the field station, the ants
build a tetrahedron from them one sliver at a time, then let it go and start
again.
