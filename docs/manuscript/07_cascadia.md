# The grove's Cascadia {#sec:cascadia}

The grove is invented; most of what lives in it is not. The field station and
the storybook take their weather, their animals and their unease from the
Pacific Northwest coast, and pieces of the real region shape what the games do.

Fog. Coast redwoods comb water out of the summer fog. The drip that falls
beneath them is a measurable input of water to the forest and to the plants
that grow under it [@dawson1998], and the trees also take fog water in through
their leaves, which keeps them from drying out [@burgess2004]. Both games keep
the fog: the field station hides its distances in it, and the storybook lets
drops fall through it.

Tremor. Beneath the Cascadia margin the subducting plate slips slowly, again
and again, in episodes that come with a faint seismic tremor
[@rogers2003]. In the field station the ground hums every so often: masts that
have been re-tensioned ring their chords, slack ones rattle, and the radio
reports in.

The great earthquake. In {{CASCADIA_QUAKE_DATE}} a great Cascadia earthquake
sent a tsunami across the Pacific to Japan, where its arrival was written down,
and those records date the earthquake and bound its size [@satake1996]. The
coast dropped, tidewater drowned the forests standing on it, and the dead
trunks of some still stand in the marshes [@atwater2005]. The turbidites that
great earthquakes shook down the continental slope record a long series of
such ruptures along the margin through the Holocene [@goldfinger2012]. The
field station's ghost forest is one of the drowned stands, with a tensegrity
mast among its flooded roots.

Light and life. From the field station's summit, with the sun at the player's
back, the patrol's shadows fall on the fog below and a glory rings one head
only, the player's own: a glory is light scattered back by small droplets
around the point opposite the sun, so each observer sees it around the shadow
of their own head [@laven2005]. Foxfire glows on the fallen logs, the light of
fungi that, in at least one species, keep their glow on a daily clock
[@oliveira2015]. A rough-skinned newt crosses the trail and arches to show its
orange underside. The newts are defended by tetrodotoxin, and the garter
snakes that eat them have evolved resistance to it unevenly across the newts'
range [@brodie2002].
